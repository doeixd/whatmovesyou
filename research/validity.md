# Validity and caveats

- **No peer-reviewed validation found (after a brief search).** I found no published reliability or validity studies for the Braverman Nature Assessment. That includes internal consistency, test-retest reliability, factor structure, and comparisons with measured neurotransmitter levels.
- **It can't measure what it claims to.** A self-report behavior questionnaire can't measure brain dopamine, acetylcholine, GABA or serotonin. There is also no accepted clinical lab test for brain neurotransmitter levels to validate it against. Urine and blood tests don't reflect brain levels.
- **The model is oversimplified.** Mapping one neurotransmitter onto one personality type and one brain wave doesn't match current neuroscience. Neurotransmitters act across many circuits and receptor types.
- **The questionnaire's design has flaws:**
  - Every item is keyed in the same direction, so people who tend to agree with statements score high everywhere.
  - Groups have different lengths (3B has 40 items; the other deficiency groups have 25).
  - Some items appear under more than one nature, e.g. "I have insomnia" (1A, 2B, 4B) and "I sleep too much" (4A, 1B).
  - Some items don't obviously relate to their nature (e.g. "I value a religious philosophy" under dopamine).
- **Clinical risk:** 4B includes suicidality items. Any app should show crisis resources (e.g. 988 in the US) when those are answered TRUE, and should not present supplement advice as treatment.
- **Commercial context:** The book pairs each deficiency with supplement protocols. Check the author's commercial ties yourself before relying on those recommendations (not researched here).
- **Reasonable framing:** present it as a self-reflection or personality questionnaire from a popular health book, not as a diagnostic tool.
