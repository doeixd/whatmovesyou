# Planning notes 3: the real knobs

Response to: *"we all know that ADHD dopamine-deficient friend, the guy with way too much testosterone, the woman who feels her feelings way too much. Our behavior is downstream of things actually happening in our brains/bodies."*

**The premise is right.** Real, present, measurable physiological differences do produce large and reliable behavioral differences between people. The previous notes ([PLAN-notes-02-reactivity.md](PLAN-notes-02-reactivity.md)) drifted toward "traits are traits, chemistry is unknowable," and that overshoots. Plenty of this *is* knowable.

**What's wrong is only the labels.** "Dopamine-deficient" is the wrong name for something that's genuinely there. The fix isn't to abandon biology — it's to use the knobs that have actually been pinned down.

---

## 1. The inclusion rule

Here's the criterion that makes this test different from Braverman's, and from every other quiz:

> **A knob goes in the test only if there's a way to check the answer.**

Every included setpoint must have at least one of:
- a **biomarker** (blood test, melatonin timing, genotype)
- a **drug/challenge response** (stimulant trial, iron repletion, hormone add-back)
- an **objective behavioral measure** (actigraphy, sleep timing, cognitive task)
- a **prospective daily-rating protocol** (the PMDD gold standard)

Braverman fails this instantly: no item on his 315 has any confirmation route. Our test's output shouldn't be "you are X." It should be **"you show the pattern of X — here's how to find out."**

That's the honest version of exactly what's being asked for: a screener that points at real, physically-present knobs *and* hands you the way to verify each one.

---

## 2. Tier A — real setpoints, questionnaire-detectable, objectively confirmable

These are the strongest candidates. Each is a genuine physiological difference, present right now in the person's body, with reliable behavioral consequences and a confirmation route.

### Circadian phase (chronotype)
**The single best proof that a questionnaire can capture a real physiological setpoint.**
- The setpoint is literal: the timing of your internal clock, measured by dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO).
- The MCTQ's corrected mid-sleep score correlates with DLMO at **r ≈ .54–.68**, and a model with MCTQ + MEQ + age explains ~60% of DLMO variance. (Correlations are weaker in some populations — high-schoolers, and rural samples on workdays.)
- Behavioral consequences are large: when you can think, when you can fall asleep, social jetlag, evening types' elevated depression and substance-use risk.
- **Confirmation:** DLMO (research), or actigraphy/wearable sleep timing on free days (consumer-accessible).
- **Actionable:** light timing, schedule shifts, melatonin timing.

### ADHD
The "ADHD friend" is real. It's the mislabeling that's the problem.
- ADHD is highly heritable, polygenic, stimulant-responsive, and validated by rating scales in clinical use.
- **But the dopamine-deficiency story doesn't hold.** A 2024 review concludes there's evidence for dopamine *involvement* but **limited evidence for a hypo-dopaminergic state** as such. A meta-analysis found striatal DAT density ~14% *higher* in ADHD with large heterogeneity — and density correlated with **prior stimulant exposure**, i.e. the finding may be a consequence of treatment, not a cause of the condition.
- **What's true:** a real, physically-present neurodevelopmental difference with a clear drug response. **What's false:** "low dopamine."
- **Confirmation:** validated rating scales (ASRS), clinical assessment, and response to a supervised stimulant trial.
- **For our test:** include an ADHD-pattern screener, name it correctly, and route to assessment rather than to supplements.

### Hormone-change sensitivity (PMDD and related)
The single best-evidenced person-level knob in all of this research.
- Add-back studies: identical hormone levels, opposite mood response in PMDD vs controls; the trigger is *change*, not level.
- **Confirmation route is unusually good:** prospective daily symptom ratings across two cycles is the actual diagnostic gold standard — and it's exactly what an app can do.
- Also covers contraception-start mood change, postpartum, perimenopause.

### Alcohol-flush genotype (ALDH2)
**The clearest case of a questionnaire detecting a genotype.**
- Self-reported flushing is 100% sensitive but only ~68% specific for ALDH2 deficiency; modified flushing questionnaires reach ~95% sensitivity / ~77% specificity.
- Behavioral consequence is direct and large: flushers drink far less, with different health risks.
- **Confirmation:** genotype, or an observed flush.
- **Why it matters for design:** proof that a couple of well-chosen questions can identify a real, discrete biological difference. That's the standard to aim for.

### Iron status / ferritin
- Low ferritin (often <50 µg/L, with or without anemia) is causally linked to restless legs; an RCT of ferric carboxymaltose in **non-anemic** iron deficiency improved RLS severity. Iron is required for dopamine synthesis — a rare place where the dopamine story is actually mechanistic.
- Also fatigue and cognitive complaints.
- **Confirmation:** a ferritin blood test. Cheap, ordinary, actionable.

### Thyroid function
- Subclinical hypothyroidism carries roughly 2.35× depression risk (pooled n = 12,315), stronger in younger people.
- **Confirmation:** TSH/free T4.
- Included mainly as the model of what an honest "deficiency" looks like: lab value, dose-response, treatment guided by the number, not by a symptom checklist.

### B12 (overt deficiency only)
- Overt deficiency produces real neurological and cognitive effects, and supplementation helps. **Subclinical** deficiency supplementation doesn't reliably improve cognition in older adults.
- Good illustration of the general rule: **deficiency effects are real at clinical thresholds and mostly vanish within the normal range.**

## 3. Tier B — real, but the honest version is narrower than the folk version

### Androgen exposure ("the guy with way too much testosterone")
This is where the folk model and the evidence diverge most sharply — in *both* directions.

**What's weak:** normal-range testosterone in adult men. Baseline T correlates with aggression at r ≈ .07. A preregistered RCT with **1,000 men** found no effect on nine economic outcomes. The "high-T guy" as a normal-variation explanation mostly doesn't survive contact with data.

**What's strong:** androgen exposure at clinical magnitudes.
- **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (high prenatal androgens): meta-analysis shows masculinized/defeminized play behavior; females with CAH show less tender-mindedness, more physical aggression, less interest in infants; males with CAH show the reverse pattern.
- **Testosterone therapy in trans men:** 4 of 7 prospective studies found increased anger-related constructs (anger expression, anger arousal), with increased libido — though notably without increased aggressive behavior, self-harm or hospitalization. Depression and anxiety symptoms dropped substantially.

**Translation:** the knob is real, and it moves behavior hard — but at *exposure* magnitudes (developmental, therapeutic, supraphysiological), not at the differences between two men in a bar. So the honest question isn't "is your T high?" but "have you had an androgen exposure event?" — puberty, TRT, steroids, transition, PCOS, CAH.

**And the useful reframe:** what people describe as the "high-T guy" is probably better captured as **high dominance-seeking + low restraint + high provocation reactivity** — measurable by questionnaire, and closer to what the testosterone literature actually finds (effects appear *under provocation*, in men already high in dominance).

### Sensitivity ("the woman who feels her feelings way too much")
This folk category is real but contains **at least four distinct things**, and separating them matters enormously because they have different implications:

| Underlying thing | What it is | Confirmation route |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory processing sensitivity** | Heritable trait; HSP scale with a bifactor structure (Ease of Excitation, Low Sensory Threshold, Aesthetic Sensitivity). Predicts stress/somatic symptoms **independent of neuroticism**. Shows *differential susceptibility*: worse outcomes in bad environments, **better** outcomes in good ones. | HSP scale; daily-diary reactivity to events |
| **Trait negative emotionality** | Neuroticism inward facets; links to 5-HT2A binding (r = .37); SSRI-responsive | IPIP/BFAS; treatment response |
| **Hormone-change sensitivity** | PMDD-type; symptoms tied to cycle phase | Prospective daily ratings across 2 cycles |
| **Emotion dysregulation** | Rapid intense shifts, interpersonal trigger, clinical-range; different treatment (DBT) entirely | Clinical assessment |

**This is a genuinely valuable output.** Someone who has been told their whole life they're "too sensitive" gets four distinguishable possibilities, one of which (SPS) is framed as an advantage in good environments rather than a defect. That's more useful than any type label.

### Caffeine metabolism (CYP1A2)
- The rs762551 variant splits people into fast (AA) and slow (AC/CC) metabolizers. Fast metabolizers get more cognitive and ergogenic benefit; slow metabolizers get more dizziness, anxiety, sleep disruption and cardiovascular strain.
- **Confirmation:** genotype (consumer genetic tests report it), or a structured self-experiment.
- Modest effects, but a clean, checkable, everyday knob.

## 4. Tier C — what to say about the rest

Braverman's four natures mostly land here: real-sounding, no confirmation route, no supporting evidence. Rather than silently dropping them, the test should say what happened to them:

- "GABA nature" → no validated trait exists. The nearest real things are anxiety (GAD-7, measurable) and benzodiazepine/alcohol response.
- "Acetylcholine nature" → no validated trait. The one cholinergic PET trait finding points to *higher harm avoidance*, not creativity.
- "Serotonin nature = impulsive thrill-seeker" → inverted. Serotonin tracks constraint; thrill-seeking tracks dopamine.
- "Dopamine nature" → closest to surviving, but the real construct is exploration/drive, not "rational and tough-minded."

## 5. How this reshapes the test

The previous two docs were converging on trait scales plus reactivity. This adds a third layer, and I think it's the one that actually delivers what's being asked for:

```
Layer 1  TRAITS      What you're like            (stable, weakly biological)
Layer 2  GAIN        What moves you              (reactivity; the PMDD insight)
Layer 3  SETPOINTS   What's physically different (checkable knobs)  ← new, and the point
```

**Layer 3 is the product's spine.** It's the honest version of "here's what's actually happening in your body," because every entry comes with a way to find out.

### What a result looks like

Not: *"You are GABA-deficient."*

But:

> **Likely: evening chronotype (high confidence)**
> Your free-day sleep midpoint is ~5:30am, in the latest ~10%. This is a physical clock-timing difference, not a discipline problem. It predicts your reported difficulty with morning work and your evening productivity peak.
> **Check it:** wearable sleep timing over 2 weeks on free days, or DLMO testing.
> **Move it:** morning light, timed melatonin, schedule negotiation.
>
> **Worth ruling out: low ferritin (moderate confidence)**
> You report leg restlessness at night, fatigue disproportionate to sleep, and you're a menstruating blood donor — three independent flags.
> **Check it:** ask for a ferritin test. Note that "normal" often means >15, while RLS research uses <50 as the relevant threshold.
>
> **Pattern present: ADHD-type attention profile (moderate confidence)**
> Scores in the range where formal assessment is usually warranted. Note: this is *not* "low dopamine" — that model isn't supported by the imaging evidence. It is a real, heritable, treatable difference.
> **Check it:** clinical assessment; ASRS is a screener, not a diagnosis.

That's specific, physical, checkable and actionable — which is what the Braverman test promised and never delivered.

---

## 6. The two rules that keep this honest

1. **Screen, then verify.** Every output is a hypothesis with a named test. Confidence language is mandatory, and "not enough signal" is a valid result.
2. **Clinical range ≠ normal range.** The pattern across everything here — testosterone, B12, thyroid, iron, vitamin D — is that effects are large at clinical extremes and shrink toward nothing within the normal range. The test should be *much* more confident about flagging possible clinical-range issues than about interpreting normal variation.

## 7. Open questions this raises

1. **Does this become a medical screener?** It's edging that way. Flagging possible thyroid/iron/ADHD issues is useful and also regulatory-adjacent. Needs a deliberate line: "suggest a conversation with a clinician," never "you have X."
2. **How many setpoints ship in v1?** Chronotype, hormone sensitivity and iron are the strongest (cheap confirmation, large behavioral effects). ADHD is high-value and high-caution.
3. **Do we integrate consumer genotype data** (23andMe exports for ALDH2, CYP1A2)? Powerful, and a large privacy/regulatory surface.
4. **Can wearable data be imported** for chronotype confirmation? That would close the screen→verify loop inside the product.
5. **What's the failure mode for the worried well?** A screener that flags five possible issues creates anxiety and unnecessary testing. Thresholds should be conservative and the copy calm.
6. **How do we handle the folk labels people arrive with?** Someone who says "I'm dopamine-deficient" needs to be met where they are and moved somewhere more accurate without being made to feel stupid. Tone matters more here than anywhere else in the product.
