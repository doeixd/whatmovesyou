# How neurotransmitters and hormones actually affect behavior

Companion to [neurotransmitters-and-personality.md](neurotransmitters-and-personality.md). That file covers *stable traits*. This one covers *behavior* — what changes when a chemical system is manipulated, which is where the causal evidence lives.

**The pattern:** manipulating brain chemistry reliably shifts *states and behaviors* (motivation, impulsivity, fear, appetite, aggression under provocation) and, over months, can shift measured traits. What it doesn't do is work in the other direction: you can't infer someone's chemistry from their behavior or from a questionnaire. Chemistry→behavior is causal and well-evidenced. Behavior→chemistry is an inference the evidence doesn't support.

Effects are also **person-dependent**: the same dose changes behavior a lot in some people and not at all in others, based on baseline state, receptor genotype, sex and diagnosis.

---

## 1. Neurotransmitters: the strongest causal evidence

### Dopamine → motivation and impulse control
- **Parkinson's drugs are the clearest natural experiment.** Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) cause impulse-control disorders: pathological gambling, compulsive shopping, hypersexuality, binge eating. The DOMINION study found these in ~14% of Parkinson's patients overall and ~17% on a dopamine agonist, 2–3× the rate of those not taking one. Higher levodopa doses add risk. Reviews put broader "impulsive behaviors" as high as ~58%.
- **What this shows:** a drug acting on one receptor family can produce a personality-scale behavior change (a lifelong non-gambler starts gambling) that reverses when the drug is withdrawn. This is the single best evidence that dopamine causally drives approach behavior in humans.
- **Amphetamine** increases willingness to expend effort for reward (Treadway 2012).
- **Caveat:** the direction is dose- and receptor-specific, not "more dopamine = more drive."

### Serotonin → aggression, impulsivity and mood, in vulnerable people
- **Meta-analysis** (175 samples, >6,500 people): central serotonin function correlates inversely with aggression, anger and hostility, r = −.12. Pharmacological challenge studies show the strongest effect (r = −.21), CSF metabolite studies the weakest (r = −.06).
- **Acute tryptophan depletion** (lowering serotonin precursor availability) increases aggression **in people already predisposed to aggression**, not in everyone. It mostly does *not* impair executive function.
- **SSRIs:** in a 240-person RCT, paroxetine reduced neuroticism and raised extraversion beyond its effect on depression (Tang 2009).
- **Psychedelics (5-HT2A agonists)** shift traits measurably. High-dose psilocybin raised Openness for over a year in people who had a mystical experience (MacLean 2011), and trials in depression and alcohol use disorder report lower neuroticism and higher extraversion, openness and conscientiousness. This is the largest measured trait change from any single intervention, though the trials are small and unblinding is a problem.

### GABA and glutamate → arousal, anxiety and disinhibition
- Benzodiazepines and alcohol act on GABA-A receptors and produce immediate anxiety reduction and behavioral disinhibition. This is the most familiar everyday evidence that a transmitter system changes behavior.
- Ketamine (NMDA/glutamate) produces rapid antidepressant effects, evidence that the glutamate system controls mood state on a timescale of hours.
- **But:** none of this means a questionnaire can detect someone's GABA level.

### Norepinephrine → arousal, vigilance and risk
- In a study manipulating both systems in military personnel, **noradrenaline** raised risk-taking, while **cortisol** did not. Cortisol sped recall and noradrenaline slowed it.
- Propranolol (beta-blocker) blunts emotional memory consolidation, which is why it's studied for trauma.

## 2. Hormones

### Testosterone: much weaker than popular accounts claim
- **Correlational:** baseline testosterone correlates with aggression at only r ≈ .07. Testosterone *increases* correlate slightly better, r ≈ .16 (2020 meta-analysis). The correlation shows up in male samples, not female ones.
- **Causal:** a 2020 meta-analysis found **no evidence of a causal link** from testosterone administration to aggression across samples.
- **Large RCTs:** a preregistered trial with **1,000 men** found **no effect** on nine economic-preference outcomes. Two further RCTs found effects on competitiveness, confidence and risk-taking "statistically indistinguishable from zero."
- **Where effects do appear:** under social provocation, and in men already high in dominance or impulsivity. The current view is that testosterone modulates *status-relevant* behavior in context rather than causing aggression.
- **Dual-hormone hypothesis** (testosterone matters only when cortisol is low): a meta-analysis of 33 studies, n = 8,538, found the interaction significant but tiny (r = −.061). The underlying literature had a median sample of 64 and about **16% statistical power**, with excess significant results. Detecting the effect properly needs n ≈ 300 per study.
- **Power posing:** the famous claim that a 2-minute pose raises testosterone and lowers cortisol **failed to replicate** in a larger study (Ranehill 2015, n = 200). Self-reported feelings of power did replicate. A good cautionary tale for any "change your chemistry in minutes" claim.

### Cortisol and stress: real but specific effects
- Hydrocortisone given after a distressing event **reduces intrusive memories**, without changing voluntary recall.
- It impairs metacognitive accuracy (knowing whether you're right) without changing task performance or making people feel stressed.
- Effects on risky decision-making are mixed. Combined cortisol and noradrenaline manipulation may reduce loss aversion, whereas either alone doesn't.
- **Takeaway:** cortisol changes how experiences are encoded and how confident people are, rather than simply making them anxious.

### Ovarian hormones: sensitivity matters more than level
- **PMDD (Schmidt et al., 1998, replicated since):** researchers shut down ovarian hormones with a GnRH agonist, then added back estradiol or progesterone blind. Women with PMDD developed mood symptoms; healthy controls did not, at identical hormone levels. A 2017 follow-up showed symptoms are triggered by *change* in hormone levels, not by stable levels.
- **This is the single most important concept for a chemistry-and-behavior app:** the behavioral effect came from individual *sensitivity to change*, not from having abnormal levels. Everyone's levels were the same.
- **Hormonal contraception:** a Danish cohort of over a million women found a small but real increase in later antidepressant use and depression diagnosis, strongest in adolescents.
- **Ovulatory shift hypothesis:** claims that women's mate preferences shift across the cycle largely **failed to replicate** in large preregistered studies (Stern et al., 2020, n = 157 over two cycles; a second study, n = 257, 1,028 sessions). Weak evidence for a general attractiveness increase, not tied to measured hormones.

### Thyroid: a genuine "deficiency" model that works
- Subclinical hypothyroidism is associated with higher depression risk (pooled n = 12,315, RR ≈ 2.35), more so in younger patients.
- This is what a real chemical deficiency looks like clinically: a **blood test**, a clear dose-response, and treatment guided by the lab value, not by a symptom questionnaire. Braverman-style deficiency scoring has no equivalent.

### Appetite and reward hormones: chemistry reaching into "willpower"
- **Ghrelin** (hunger) increases neural responses to food cues, boosts motivation to eat without making food taste better, and lowers sensitivity to negative feedback.
- **Leptin:** leptin-deficient people show heightened reward-region responses to food cues even when full, which leptin normalizes.
- **GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide):** a phase-2 RCT reduced alcohol consumption and craving in people with alcohol use disorder and also cut cigarettes per day in smokers. A 26-week Lancet RCT in people with alcohol use disorder and obesity showed robust effects. Phase-3 trials are running.
- **Why this matters:** behavior usually explained as self-control turns out to be adjustable with a metabolic hormone, and the effect crosses substances (alcohol *and* nicotine), which points at a shared reward pathway.

### Oxytocin: the cautionary case
- Early intranasal oxytocin findings on trust and social cognition largely didn't replicate. A registered report with equivalence testing found **no meaningful effect on trust**, and meta-analyses find no reliable effect on emotion recognition in neurodevelopmental disorders.

## 3. What generalizes

1. **Causation runs one way in the evidence.** Drugs and hormones change behavior. That does not license inferring chemistry from behavior.
2. **Sensitivity beats level.** PMDD, dopamine-agonist impulse disorders and tryptophan depletion all show the same shape: identical exposure, wildly different behavioral response depending on the person. A "level" model, which is what Braverman uses, misses this entirely.
3. **Context gates the effect.** Testosterone shows up under provocation. Tryptophan depletion shows up in aggression-prone people. Ghrelin shows up around food cues.
4. **Effects are state-like and often reversible.** Stop the agonist, the gambling stops.
5. **Effect sizes in healthy people are small.** Big effects come from clinical populations, drugs, and disease, not from normal variation.
6. **Popular "hack your hormones" claims have a bad replication record.** Power posing, oxytocin sprays and ovulatory shifts are the well-documented examples.

## 4. Implications for the app

- Frame anything chemical as **"this drug/hormone changes this behavior"**, sourced, rather than "your score means your chemical is low."
- If deficiency framing is used at all, model it on **thyroid**: a lab test, not a questionnaire.
- An honest, useful feature is **tracking**: log sleep, mood, alcohol, cycle phase, medication and symptoms over time and show within-person patterns. That respects the "sensitivity to change" finding, and it's something an app can genuinely do.
- Avoid claiming any quick fix raises or lowers a transmitter. That's the power-posing error.
