# Planning notes 4: mental-illness traits as dimensions everyone sits on

Response to: *"mental illnesses are axes/spectrums everyone lies on — more 'autistic' or 'schizoid' or 'ADHD' or 'Type A' or 'depressive' or 'manic' personalities. This is real and should be on the test."*

**This isn't a hunch — it's the mainstream position in psychopathology research**, and it's better evidenced than anything in Braverman. It also solves a problem the earlier notes had: the trait layer was shaping up as a bland Big Five clone. Clinical spectra are what people actually notice about each other, and they're measurable.

---

## 1. The evidence that these are dimensions, not categories

- **Haslam et al. taxometric meta-analysis:** 317 findings from 183 articles, combined n ≈ 533,000. Findings supporting **dimensional models outnumbered categorical ones 5 to 1**. Crucially, categorical ("taxonic") findings were *less* common in the more recent and methodologically stronger studies. No content domain showed aggregate support for a categorical model — and psychopathology was no more categorical than ordinary personality variation.
- **HiTOP** (Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology) is the consortium-built dimensional alternative to DSM categories. Its rationale: categorical diagnosis has limited reliability, arbitrary and unstable cutoffs, excessive comorbidity, within-diagnosis heterogeneity and poor coverage. **No common mental disorder has ever been empirically verified as a categorical entity**, and binary diagnoses are *less* reliable and valid than dimensional severity scores of the same content.
- **DSM-5 itself half-adopted this** with the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (Section III) and its trait inventory.

So the claim "everyone sits somewhere on these axes" is not a folk simplification of clinical categories. It's closer to the truth than the categories are.

---

## 2. The structure to build on

HiTOP's spectra and the DSM-5 trait model converge on roughly the same five-to-six axes. The **PID-5** (Personality Inventory for DSM-5) operationalizes them, with a replicated five-domain / 25-facet structure validated across many languages:

| Axis | PID-5 domain | Folk version | Big Five relative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative emotionality | **Negative Affectivity** | "depressive," "anxious," "feels everything" | Neuroticism ↑ |
| Social withdrawal | **Detachment** | "schizoid," "loner," "cold" | Extraversion ↓ |
| Callous/hostile | **Antagonism** | "difficult," part of "Type A" | Agreeableness ↓ |
| Impulsivity/distractibility | **Disinhibition** | "ADHD," "chaotic" | Conscientiousness ↓ |
| Odd/unusual cognition | **Psychoticism** | "schizotypal," "out there" | Openness (maladaptive) |

Versions: PID-5 (220 items), PID-5-SF (100), **PID-5-BF (25)**. The brief form is short enough for a consumer product.

Plus dimensions HiTOP/PID-5 don't cover well, which need their own instruments:

| Axis | Instrument | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Autistic traits** | AQ (or newer: CATI, RAADS-R) | Traits continuously distributed in the general population; broader autism phenotype established in relatives. **But** AQ's factor structure is unstable across samples — a 35-item 5-factor solution performs best and holds up in both community and clinic samples. |
| **Schizotypy** | SPQ, O-LIFE | Strong. An 8-year multiwave study showed positive schizotypy predicts later positive symptoms; negative schizotypy predicts negative symptoms, schizoid traits, and impaired social/global functioning. Three replicated sub-dimensions: positive, negative, disorganized. |
| **Hypomanic / cyclothymic temperament** | HPS, TEMPS-A | Strong. In the 23-year Northern Finland Birth Cohort follow-up, **high HPS was the best predictor of later bipolar disorder (d = 0.67)**. TEMPS-A measures cyclothymic, dysthymic, irritable, hyperthymic and anxious temperaments; cyclothymic has the most solid evidence. |
| **ADHD traits** | ASRS (WHO, free) | Dimensional, heritable, treatment-relevant. See [PLAN-notes-03-setpoints.md](PLAN-notes-03-setpoints.md) for why "dopamine-deficient" is the wrong label for it. |

### The one that doesn't make the cut: Type A

Worth including as an explicit exclusion, because it's the folk axis everyone cites. The original Friedman-Rosenman Type A → heart disease finding **failed to replicate** across subsequent studies. Only the *hostility* component retained any link to coronary disease, and even that evidence is regarded as thin. Hostility is already covered by PID-5 Antagonism. "Type A" as a personality type should be retired, and saying so in the report is a credibility win — it shows the test drops things that don't survive scrutiny.

## 3. Where this actually fits: it replaces Layer 1

The three-layer model from the last note becomes:

```
Layer 1  SPECTRA     Where you sit on dimensions that run into clinical territory   ← now this
Layer 2  GAIN        What moves you (reactivity)
Layer 3  SETPOINTS   What's physically different and checkable
```

This is a strict upgrade on the earlier "four trait dimensions" plan:
- Better evidenced (Haslam, HiTOP, decades of instrument validation)
- More interesting to users (matches the categories people actually use about each other)
- Clinically meaningful without being diagnostic
- Free or cheap instruments already exist, so we're not inventing scales from scratch

**And one of these axes has the best neurotransmitter evidence in the entire research folder:** Psychoticism/thought-disorder ↔ dopamine. Antipsychotics work by D2 blockade; amphetamine can induce psychosis; the dose-response is established. That link is *far* stronger than anything supporting a "dopamine personality type." Ironically, the place dopamine genuinely explains personality-level variation is the axis Braverman never mentions.

Rough mapping from spectra to the mechanism layers:

| Spectrum | Mechanism link | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Psychoticism | Dopamine (D2) | **Strong** |
| Disinhibition | Dopamine reward/effort; ICDs on agonists | **Strong** |
| Negative Affectivity | 5-HT2A binding; SSRI response; stress reactivity | **Moderate** |
| Detachment | μ-opioid and attachment | Preliminary |
| Antagonism | Serotonin-aggression (r = −.12); androgen exposure | Weak-moderate |
| Autistic traits | Highly heritable; no clean transmitter story | Genetic, not chemical |

## 4. The critical design problem: trait ≠ disorder

This is where a test like this goes wrong, and it's the thing to get right.

**Dimensional does not mean "everyone is a bit autistic/bipolar/psychotic."** Two specific reasons:

1. **Impairment is a separate axis.** Someone can be high on autistic traits with an excellent life, and someone else moderately elevated can be severely impaired. The clinical threshold isn't a trait cutoff — it's *trait × distress × functional impact*. Any honest version of this test needs a **functioning measure** (e.g. a WSAS-style 5-item impairment scale) crossed with every spectrum score.

2. **The extremes may be etiologically different.** Continuity of *traits* doesn't guarantee continuity of *causes*. In autism especially, severe presentations are enriched for rare de novo variants that aren't just "more of" common-variant trait loading. So the honest statement is: traits are continuous; the causes at the far end may not be the same causes.

**Proposed output grid:**

|  | Low impairment | High impairment |
|---|---|---|
| **Trait elevated** | "This is a real feature of how you're built. Here's what it costs and what it's good for." | "This pattern is affecting your life. Worth a formal assessment." |
| **Trait typical** | — | "Something's affecting you that this profile doesn't explain. Worth looking further." |

That bottom-right cell matters: it catches people whose problem isn't in their personality profile at all.

## 5. Framing the axes fairly

Each spectrum has a genuine upside, and stating it isn't a consolation prize — it's accurate.

| Axis | Cost | Genuine upside |
|---|---|---|
| Autistic traits | Social friction, transition difficulty, sensory overload | Pattern detection, systematic thinking, honesty, sustained deep focus |
| Detachment/schizoid | Isolation, missed support | Independence, unaffected by social pressure, comfort alone |
| Psychoticism/schizotypy | Misreading situations, paranoia risk | Original associations, creativity (the positive-schizotypy/creativity link is one of the better-supported findings here) |
| Disinhibition/ADHD | Missed follow-through, impulsive costs | Crisis performance, idea generation, hyperfocus |
| Negative affectivity | Suffering, rumination | Threat detection, conscientious preparation, emotional depth |
| Hypomanic temperament | Overcommitment, crash, risk | Drive, charisma, productivity in bursts |
| Antagonism | Damaged relationships | Negotiation, not folding under pressure |

The **sensory-processing-sensitivity** finding from the last note generalizes here: differential susceptibility means high-sensitivity people do *worse* in bad environments and *better* in good ones. A cost-and-benefit framing is the evidence-based one, not a euphemism.

## 6. Risks — this is the highest-risk part of the product

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-diagnosis** | "The test said I'm autistic/bipolar." | Never name a disorder as a result. Name the *trait dimension*. Elevated + impaired → route to assessment, explicitly "this is not a diagnosis." |
| **False reassurance** | Someone genuinely unwell scores mid-range and stops looking | The "trait typical + high impairment" cell must exist and be prominent |
| **Cyberchondria** | Seven axes means something is always elevated; that's how percentiles work | Report only genuinely notable scores; calm copy; explain that ~1 in 6 people is above the 84th percentile on any axis, by definition |
| **Stigma / identity lock-in** | "I'm a schizoid" becomes a self-concept | Language of *degree and current state*, not identity. Note that these scores change over time. |
| **Suicide/psychosis risk items** | Some instruments include them | Crisis resources, same as [PLAN-next-gen-test.md](PLAN-next-gen-test.md) §7 |
| **Regulatory** | Screening for disorders edges toward medical-device territory | Wellness framing, no diagnostic claims, no treatment recommendations |

A hard rule worth adopting: **the test never outputs a disorder name as a finding about the user.** It outputs a percentile on a named trait dimension plus an impairment reading, and only ever suggests *assessment*.

## 8. What v1 might contain

- **PID-5-BF (25 items)** → five spectra, normed, short
- **ASRS-6 screener** → ADHD traits
- **Short autistic-traits scale** → prefer a well-behaved one over the full AQ given its factor instability
- **Schizotypy short form** → positive/negative/disorganized
- **HPS short form** → hypomanic temperament
- **WSAS-style impairment scale (5 items)** → the cross-cutting axis that makes everything else interpretable

That's roughly 70–90 items, about 10 minutes, and it covers every folk category in the original question with validated instruments — except Type A, which gets an explanation of why it's absent.

## 9. Open questions

1. **Does this swallow the whole product?** The spectra layer is now more compelling than the reactivity and setpoint layers. Is the test *primarily* a dimensional psychopathology profile, with mechanism as a secondary layer? Possibly — and that might be the right answer.
2. **Licensed instruments or original items?** Licensed means instant validity and norms, plus legal work and no differentiation. Original means freedom and a long validation road.
3. **How many axes before it's overwhelming?** Six spectra + impairment is probably the ceiling for a consumer report.
4. **Do we show percentiles for clinical-range scores?** Telling someone they're at the 97th percentile on psychoticism is heavy. Bands may be kinder and more honest than numbers.
5. **Teens?** These scales behave differently in adolescents, and self-diagnosis risk is highest there. Probably adults-only at launch.
6. **Does the folk-label bridge help or hurt?** Saying "this is what people mean by 'schizoid'" aids comprehension and risks reinforcing stigma. Test both phrasings with real readers.
